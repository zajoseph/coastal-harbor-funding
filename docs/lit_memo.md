# T0.2 Literature review and positioning memo

Coastal Harbor Funding Equity · 2026-11-17 · Task T0.2

**Access key used throughout:** **[F]** = full text or official document opened; **[A]** = abstract or landing page only (paywalled or not retrieved); **[S]** = seen only in a search excerpt or another work's reference list, not opened. Claims resting on [A]/[S] sources are worded at the level of the abstract.

## Bottom line

1. Capacity predicting grant receipt is well established, but almost all of the evidence uses **municipalities, counties or states** and **generic need** (income, poverty, social vulnerability). Nobody has studied **harbor owners** (port districts, which are special districts) against **infrastructure-specific need**.
2. No study compares the capacity gradient **across funding channels** (competitive vs. appropriated vs. earmark) for the same units. The USACE literature says the appropriated channel allocates mainly by **cargo tonnage**, which is neither "capacity" nor fishing "need". That makes H2 sharper, but it also complicates it.
3. The best application-stage evidence (NRDC on BRIC) suggests the gap opens **at application, not selection**. If that's right, models that look only at awards will understate capacity bias. H3 matters more than the plan assumes, and a public PIDP applicant list may exist (see §c).
4. The "IIJA era" (FY2022–26) is **not one policy regime**: BRIC was terminated April 2025 to March 2026 and restarted without its equity provisions; PIDP's small-port set-aside changed definition twice. H4 needs a revised period split (flag 3).

## (a) Capacity and grant allocation

**Core finding.** In competitive allocation, local administrative capacity predicts access, and the neediest places are often the least able to win. Collins & Gerber (2006) frame access as a matter of intergovernmental transaction costs. In state-run non-entitlement CDBG, they find local capacity strongly shapes access, and state institutional design can soften the effect [A]. Collins & Gerber (2008) compare four administration models (competition vs. formula for picking grantees and setting amounts). They conclude that equity is better served when grantors don't rely solely on competition [A]. Collins, Andrew & Khunwishit (2016) study 250 small municipalities (NE-CDBG, 2000–03, zero-inflated negative binomial). Contracting obstacles distort needs-based access, and low/moderate-income population does not track grant counts as expected [A]. Lee (2019) finds that HUD Continuum of Care funding rises with local capacity and falls with local need [A]. Hall (2008) gives the standard internal/external capacity framework for "grant-getting" [S]. Hall (2010) studies Kentucky counties (1993–2003). He builds capacity factors and finds that grant receipts per person differ along the rural–urban continuum, net of need [A].

**Beyond local government.** Manna & Ryan (2011): state capacity predicted both whether states applied for Race to the Top and how well they scored [A].

**IIJA-era evidence.** Hermans & Rajninger (Urban Institute, 2024) use FY2022 awards from 29 IIJA competitive programs plus CoC, in counties over 50,000 people, with Census of Governments capacity measures [F]:
- $10 per capita more in transportation payroll goes with an 18.1-point higher probability of winning a competitive transportation award.
- Among winners, each $1 per capita of transportation payroll goes with $5.78 per capita more funding.
- The authors make no causal claim. The data cover a single year, and small counties are excluded.

**Allocation vs. implementation.** A parallel literature links capacity to *post-award* performance (Terman & Feiock 2015 [A]; Shybalkina 2024 [A]). Agencies know this. EDA lists applicant "financial or management capacity" among its selection objectives (GAO-14-131) [F]. So part of any capacity gradient is designed in, not bias. That matters for how we word H1 (flag 1).

## (b) Program-specific evidence

**BRIC / FEMA hazard mitigation.** This is the densest equity literature, but it is mostly NGO and government work.
- **Headwaters Economics (Smith 2022, 2023) [F]:**
  - FY2020: ~94% of BRIC grants went to coastal states and wealthier counties.
  - Across three years, only 13% of counties with funded national-competition projects (18 of 138) scored below the national median on the Rural Capacity Index.
  - Year 3: 70 high-capacity counties received 83% of national-competition dollars.
- **GAO-26-107774 (Sept 2026) [F]:**
  - FY2020–23: 3,820 subapplications, about $16B requested against ~$4.8B available; 1,245 subgrants.
  - National-competition award rate about 13%.
  - Special governmental districts (the category port districts fall into) submitted 216 subapplications and had a 27% award rate. Economically disadvantaged rural communities had 32%.
  - Communities reported heavy reliance on consultants and hundreds of staff hours per subapplication.
- **Earlier HMA evidence:** GAO-21-140 [F]: 8 of 12 jurisdictions visited cited applicants' technical capacity as a barrier. Seong, Losey & Gu (2022) find HMGP assistance per recipient is higher in urban counties, net of vulnerability (1989–2018) [A]. FEMA's own HMGP equity baseline (2024) reports 61.7% of obligations went to CEJST-disadvantaged areas. It doesn't analyze capacity [F].

**PIDP (MARAD).** We found **no peer-reviewed or GAO analysis of who wins PIDP**.
- GAO-22-104630 (2021) [F] reviewed port funding across seven programs (FY2010–20). Small ports may lack the organizational capacity to apply, while large ports have in-house grant writers. Benefit–cost analysis often requires consultants, and RAISE minimum award sizes exclude many small ports.
- The small-port set-aside keeps changing. The FY2021 NDAA cut the reserved share from 25% to 18%, switched from a project-size test to a port-size test (<8M tons), and dropped the cost-benefit requirement for eligible small ports (Kearns 2021) [F]. FY2026 materials describe ≥25% for "small projects at small ports" (trade press [S]). I couldn't verify the statutory step back to 25%.

**EDA Public Works.** GAO-14-131 [F] covers FY2007 and FY2011. 84–90% of Public Works projects were in counties at or below national per capita income, so funded places do look distressed. But funded projects were disproportionately nonrural relative to the rural population. We found no recent peer-reviewed study of EDA allocation and capacity.

**USACE navigation (appropriated channel).**
- Hird (1991) [A] is the anchor. Pork-barrel influence exists and is costly, but efficiency (benefit–cost) and equity also shape Corps project selection.
- GAO-25-107447 (2025) [F] is directly on point:
  - The Corps budgets navigation O&M mainly by tonnage and sorts coastal projects into high (>10M short tons), moderate and low (<1M) use, concentrating funds on high- and moderate-use projects.
  - Commercial fish landings are not counted in tonnage, and the Corps has no project-to-NMFS-port crosswalk.
  - Stakeholders say small fishing ports are a low priority.
- GAO-17-635 [S] reports the same tonnage-first practice for inland harbors.
- Statute softens this. WRDA 2014 set a 10% emerging-harbor minimum for HMTF spending (CRS IF11645) [F]. WRDA 2020 §102 raised it to not less than 15%, effective FY2023 [F]. The FY2023 NDAA (§8131) requires ranking criteria for small and remote harbors [F].
- Data caveat: GAO-15-810 found Corps dredging cost data unreliable [S].

**Earmarks.**
- Pre-moratorium: Lazarus (2010) finds that both chamber position and constituent-demand factors predict House earmarks (FY2008) [A].
- Post-2021 Community Project Funding:
  - Cassella, Fagan & Theriault (2023) analyze FY2022 House requests. They find partisan asymmetries and only "limited" evidence that requests reflect district type [A].
  - GAO counts $15.3B across 7,234 provisions in FY2023 (GAO-23-106561) [S].
  - Earmarks also run through competitive accounts: 68 BRIC projects ($154M) in FY2022 (Taxpayers for Common Sense) [S], and $64.7M of FY2026 PIDP appropriations designated for CPF (MARAD webinar) [S]. This matches the pdm_cpf row already in the decisions log.
- We found **no study of harbor or port earmarks** in the CPF era.

## (c) Application-stage evidence

- **NRDC on BRIC/FMA [F]:**
  - The lowest-capacity localities are less likely to apply at all.
  - In a later logit on FY2020–22 subapplications, *lower*-capacity counties (FY2020, FY2022) and higher-vulnerability counties (all years) were more likely to be selected for further review. Note that this outcome is not the final award.
  - Read together: the capacity gap is created at entry, and selection may slightly offset it. This is H3's mechanism, but NRDC uses county assignment from project titles and doesn't publish full models.
- **Political capacity at entry.** McLaughlin, Finocchiaro & Crespin (2024) [F, open access] use BUILD/RAISE applications 2009–22. States apply more aggressively when their senators are in the majority, and more efficiently when a senator holds a key committee post. So political capacity also works through applications, not only through earmarks (flag 7).
- **Who applies to RAISE.** Freemark et al. (Urban, 2024) [A, landing page only] report that RAISE applications come disproportionately from higher-income counties.
- **Data availability (relevant to T0.4/T2.5).**
  - DOT publishes TIGER–BUILD applications for 2009–2025 [F].
  - An OMB clearance for a MARAD applicant survey says MARAD keeps a list of all PIDP applications: 511 applications for 2019–23 from 248 unique applicants [F].
  - Grant listings mirroring MARAD's page mention a "Consolidated PIDP 2019–2024 Application List" [S]. **If that list is public, H3 is feasible for PIDP without FOIA.**

## (d) The gap this project fills, mapped to hypotheses

| Hyp. | What's known | What's missing / our contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **H1** capacity bias | Robust capacity–receipt association for cities, counties, states (Collins & Gerber; Hall; Urban 2024; BRIC NGO work) | No harbor-level unit; port districts rarely studied; need is generic (SVI, income). We add **harbor-specific need** (landings, channel condition, service gaps) and **owner-level capacity**, panel FY2010–26 |
| **H2** channel difference | Separate literatures: competitive grants (capacity), USACE (tonnage + politics; Hird; GAO-25-107447), earmarks (political position; Lazarus) | **No same-unit, cross-channel comparison.** Novel, but USACE's tonnage rule means the appropriated channel's "need" is cargo, so we must model tonnage explicitly |
| **H3** application stage | NRDC (BRIC), Manna & Ryan, McLaughlin et al.: entry is where capacity and politics act | Only descriptive or NGO evidence for infrastructure programs; **a formal apply→win model on PIDP applicants** would be new |
| **H4** policy levers | Set-asides and BCA waivers described (GAO-22-104630; Kearns 2021; GAO-26-107774); NRDC sees BRIC access shift after FY2020 | **No causal test** of PIDP small-port set-asides or IIJA-era changes; we can add USACE emerging-harbor floor (WRDA 2020) as a second lever |

## (e) Closest work we must engage

1. **Collins & Gerber (2006)**: the canonical capacity → access argument and the formula/competition design lever (H1, H4).
2. **Hermans & Rajninger (2024, Urban Institute)**: IIJA-era capacity measures from the Census of Governments, nearly identical to our T2.7 plan. We must show what harbor-level data add (H1).
3. **NRDC BRIC applicant and selection analyses (c. 2023–24)**: the closest H3 design, and the source of the "entry, not selection" result.
4. **McLaughlin, Finocchiaro & Crespin (2024)**: political capacity shapes applications. This challenges a clean "administrative vs. political" split in H2.
5. **Hird (1991) together with GAO-25-107447 (2025)**: how USACE actually allocates (benefit–cost, politics, tonnage), and that fishing landings are invisible to it (H2).

**Implications for T2.6 and T2.7:**
- Census of Governments payroll and staff will work for counties and cities but may not isolate port districts. Plan on owner financial statements for owner-level capacity.
- Use the RCI as a county-level robustness measure.
- Add tonnage (WCSC) as a separate need and economic covariate.
- Add political-capacity covariates in every channel.

## Findings that threaten a hypothesis or suggest a design change

1. **Capacity is partly a legitimate selection criterion (H1 framing).** EDA scores applicant capacity, and capacity predicts implementation. Recommend wording H1 as capacity *beyond* need and project merit, and adding readiness proxies where available.
2. **Award-only models may hide the bias (H1 and H3).** NRDC finds lower-capacity counties do *better* once they apply. A gradient estimated on awards alone could be flat or negative even when entry is strongly biased. The hurdle model's first stage (any award) mixes "didn't apply" with "lost". Without H3 data, the H1 interpretation is limited.
3. **The IIJA era is not one regime (H4).**
   - BRIC made no awards April 2025 to March 2026.
   - The FY2024–25 NOFO dropped the Justice40 goal and the reduced-BCA path for disadvantaged communities, ended non-financial technical assistance, and barred planning not tied to a specific project (GAO-26-107774). Its awards will mostly fall after FY2026.
   - IIJA PIDP money ends with FY2026.
   - **Proposed split:** pre-IIJA (≤FY2021) / IIJA with equity provisions (FY2022–24) / rollback (FY2025–26). Treat BRIC FY2024+ as right-censored. *This changes H4, so it needs PM and user sign-off.*
4. **The PIDP set-aside treatment varies over time (H4).** Coding must follow statute by year: 25% project-size test → 18% port-size test (FY2021 NDAA) → cost-effectiveness waiver (FY2024 NDAA) → 25% in FY2026 materials. Ask T0.3 to add a set-aside rule column by fiscal year.
5. **USACE allocates by tonnage, not by fishing need (H2).** Most Pacific fishing harbors will be "low use". A low USACE capacity gradient may reflect the tonnage rule, not need-responsiveness. Recommend adding tonnage as a covariate and treating the WRDA 2020 15% emerging-harbor floor (from FY2023) as a second H4 lever.
6. **Earmarks run through competitive accounts.** BRIC CPF and PIDP CPF dollars must be separated from competitive awards (consistent with the existing pdm_cpf decision; extend it to PIDP).
7. **Political capacity also drives competitive applications (H2).** Include congressional-representation covariates in the competitive-channel models too, or "administrative vs. political" will be confounded.
